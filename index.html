Keeping a qubit alive

A qubit cannot be copied and cannot be looked at, so both of the things that make classical error correction easy are off the table. Quantum error correction is the workaround. Everything below is a real stabilizer simulator running in your browser, not a recording of one.

01

The problem

Protecting classical information is easy because you are allowed to cheat. Store three copies of a bit, read all three, take the majority. Both moves, copying and reading, are free.

Neither is available for a qubit. The no-cloning theorem forbids duplicating an unknown quantum state, so there is nothing to take a majority over. And measuring a qubit collapses it: the superposition you were trying to protect is destroyed by the act of checking on it. A qubit in the state you care about is a qubit you are not allowed to inspect.

Worse, the errors are continuous. A classical bit fails in one way: it flips. A qubit can rotate by any angle on the Bloch sphere, in any direction, so there are infinitely many ways for it to be slightly wrong.

Quantum error correction gets around all three at once, and the trick is the same each time: never look at the data. Look only at relationships between qubits.

02

Parity, not state

Here is the move that makes everything else possible. Instead of measuring a qubit, measure whether a group of qubits agrees with itself, which is their parity. The answer is a single bit, even or odd. It says nothing about what any individual qubit holds, so it collapses nothing you cared about.

Four qubits, one measurement. Click them.

The measurement is safe because it is blind, and being blind, it leaves you with an ambiguity you will have to resolve some other way. That ambiguity comes back in section 05 as the central difficulty of the whole field.

On continuous errors This also solves the third problem. Measuring a stabilizer projects a continuously rotated qubit onto either "no error" or "a discrete Pauli error". The infinitely many possible rotations collapse into a handful of cases the moment you check, so correcting X, Y, and Z is enough to correct everything.
03

Anatomy of a patch

One parity check protects nothing on its own. Tile the plane with them and something useful appears. The rotated surface code puts d² data qubits on a grid and draws a checkerboard of parity checks across it: Z-plaquettes that catch bit flips, X-plaquettes that catch phase flips.

There are d² − 1 checks constraining d² qubits. The single degree of freedom left over is the logical qubit. It is spread across the whole patch and stored in no particular place, so damaging any particular place cannot destroy it.

Hover anything below to see what watches what.

The distance d is the length of the shortest path across the patch, and it sets how many individual failures it takes to destroy the logical qubit: at least d of them, arranged in a line. Everything after this is about making that line unlikely.

04

Errors and syndromes

Now break it. Click a data qubit to inject an error and watch which checks fire. The pattern of fired checks is called the syndrome, and it is the only information you ever get. The errors themselves are, by construction, invisible.

Then press Grow a chain and watch what happens. A run of adjacent errors lights up only its two endpoints. Every check in the middle sees two errors, reads even parity, and stays quiet.

The rest of the page is built on this fact: the syndrome sees the boundary of an error chain, never the chain itself. Two defects tell you that something connects them, not what.

05

Decoding is a guess

A decoder takes the syndrome and proposes a correction: some chain of errors with the same endpoints. Any such chain will silence every check. The decoder has no way to know whether it picked the one that actually happened, so it picks the most probable one, which is usually the shortest.

The surprising part is that it does not need to be right. Apply the correction on top of the true error and look at what is left over, the residual. If the residual closes into loops, it acts trivially on the logical qubit and you have won despite guessing wrong. You have only lost if the residual runs all the way across the patch, and in that case every check reads quiet while the answer is wrong.

Inject errors, decode, and switch the view to Residual.

Press Long chain a few times at d = 3. Once the chain is longer than half the patch, the shortest way to close it is the wrong way round, the decoder takes it, and the residual becomes a logical operator. That is what a logical error is: the decoder finds a consistent correction, and it is the wrong one.

The three decoders trade accuracy against speed. Union-Find grows clusters until each contains an even number of defects and peels them, in near-linear time. Exact minimum-weight perfect matching finds the optimal pairing and costs a great deal more. Greedy just pairs nearest neighbours and is the fastest and the worst.

06

When the readouts lie

Everything so far assumed the syndrome was trustworthy. It is not. The parity checks are themselves small quantum circuits on real hardware, and they misreport. A single lying readout is indistinguishable from a real error, and "correcting" an error that never happened introduces one.

The fix is to measure repeatedly and decode the whole history at once. What the decoder actually matches is not the raw readings but the places they change between one round and the next, called detection events. Seen that way, the two failure modes separate. A data error flips a check once and leaves it flipped, giving two events side by side in one layer. A lying readout disagrees with itself for a single round, giving two events on the same check in consecutive layers: a pair separated in time rather than in space.

The plaquettes below light on detection events, so this is the decoder's own view. With a single round the two notions coincide, which is why the earlier figures did not have to draw the distinction.

Click a plaquette below to make its readout lie in that round.

This costs d rounds of measurement per logical operation, and a decoding problem that grows in three dimensions instead of two. There is no way around it. Every figure before this one was describing an idealisation.

07

The threshold

Everything above is machinery. This is the question it exists to answer: if I add more physical qubits, does the logical qubit get better?

The answer is not always yes. A bigger patch corrects longer error chains, but it also has more qubits to go wrong and more checks to misreport. Below some critical physical error rate the first effect wins and logical error falls off exponentially in d. Above it, the second wins and adding qubits makes things worse. That crossing point is the threshold.

The table below is measured in your browser right now. Read the bottom row of each block: it compares d = 3 against d = 7 and reports which side of the threshold that column is on, or says so when the two are within each other's error bars, which is what the threshold itself looks like.

Notice that the two noise models cross over in different places. Perfect measurements buy you a much higher threshold than faulty ones, which is why the number to quote for real hardware is always the phenomenological or circuit-level one.

To locate the crossing precisely, sweep p across several distances and fit. Near threshold the curves for every distance collapse onto a single universal curve when plotted against (p − p_th) · d1/ν; the pair of values that makes the collapse tightest is the threshold and its critical exponent.

That statement is exactly true only for infinitely large patches. These are not large (the smallest is nine qubits), and the approach to the limit is not a rounding error. Fit the bare collapse to synthetic data with a threshold known in advance and it comes back 27% high, and stays there however many shots you spend, because it is bias rather than noise. So the fit carries the leading correction as well, + D · d−ω, which lets the curves approach a common shape rather than requiring them to already lie on one. Separating that term from a shift in the threshold needs at least four distances; with three, the panel says so and reports the uncorrected value.

Reading the fit The exponent ν is reported only when the sweep can actually pin it down, which is not always. Fed synthetic data with ν fixed at 1.46 in advance, the fit recovers it as 1.46 ± 0.02 from a data-noise sweep and as 0.63 ± 0.36 from a circuit-level one, and reports a tight interval either way. Its interval covers the true value 80% of the time for data noise and 46% for circuit-level, so the interval cannot be trusted to catch the problem itself: the failure is bias, and a bootstrap resamples around its own answer. Where the sweep is too thin, the panel says not determined rather than printing a number that would look like a measurement.

The reduced χ² tells you whether the collapse is believable. Near 1 means the scaling ansatz describes the data within its error bars; much larger means the fit is being forced. Here it is not only a diagnostic, it also chooses how much of the sweep to fit. The scaling form is an expansion about the threshold and stops describing points far from it, so the fit takes the widest window the form still fits and reports what fraction that was. Picking a fraction by hand makes the threshold depend on whoever picked it: one fixed choice gave a reduced χ² of 2.3 for data noise and 13.2 for phenomenological, the same number fitting one model acceptably and another not at all.

The interval is a bootstrap: the shots are resampled and the whole fit re-run a hundred-odd times, and the middle 95% of the answers is reported. That gives a wider but more accurate bar than repeating the sweep and taking the spread, because a fit biased by its own window reproduces that bias on every repeat, so the spread would look tight and be wrong. Precision here is limited by shots rather than by the method: on synthetic data the fit's error falls from 40% at 1,200 shots per point to 13% at 20,000, which is why data noise sweeps far more shots than circuit-level can afford.
08

Biased noise

Real devices do not produce X, Y, and Z errors in equal measure. In most superconducting and spin qubits, dephasing dominates, and Z errors can be one or two orders of magnitude more common than bit flips. A code designed for symmetric noise ignores that structure.

The XZZX variant alternates X and Z operators around every plaquette instead of using separate X-type and Z-type checks. The published result is that under strong bias its decoding problem decouples into near-independent one-dimensional chains, lifting the threshold substantially.

It took three fixes to get this simulator to show it. Its XZZX decoder used to match one defect set twice, on two graphs with different edge mappings, so a pure X error came back with invented Z corrections attached, and more of them the larger the patch. And the logical-operator check used a hard-coded string of Paulis that was simply wrong, so it reported failures for residuals that were not logical operators at all, at every distance. Both are fixed: the two error families are matched together in one pass, and whether a residual is a logical error is now decided by asking the stabilizer group directly.

The result is the one the literature describes. Run the comparison: at strong bias XZZX pulls clearly ahead of the rotated code, and both now improve as the patch grows.

09

The bench

That is the whole argument. This is the instrument it was describing, with every parameter exposed, including the ones the narrative had no use for: located losses, spatial burst noise, and slow temporal drift.

On circuit-level noise Circuit-level is the strictest of the three models and was the last to work. Faults land on individual gates, so a fault on an ancilla midway through its CNOTs propagates onto several data qubits at once, and the decoder needs a map of the circuit to make sense of that, not the phenomenological graph the other two use. It now decodes against a detector error model derived from the circuit itself. The check that it works is exhaustive rather than statistical: every single fault the circuit admits is corrected, at every distance, with both decoders. Section 10 has the detail.
10

Under the hood

Nothing on this page is precomputed. Every number was produced by a Rust stabilizer simulator compiled to WebAssembly and executed on your machine while you read.

Unless they say otherwise, figures quoted below were measured at bias η = 0.5 (equal parts X, Y and Z, the setting every panel here starts from) over T = d rounds. The bias matters more than it looks. At d = 7, p = 0.3% circuit-level the rotated code reads 1.07% at η = 1 and 2.37% at η = 100, while XZZX at those same two settings reads 1.08% and 0.25%. Same p, and the ranking between the codes reverses. Thresholds come from a collapse fit carrying the leading correction to scaling, with a bootstrap interval. An earlier version of this page quoted the uncorrected fit and treated its disagreement with the raw crossings as irreducible uncertainty; it was bias, and both numbers sat low. See section 7.

Stabilizer engine
A packed binary symplectic tableau. By the Gottesman–Knill theorem, Clifford circuits on n qubits are classically simulable in polynomial time; propagating Paulis through the tableau costs O(n²) and reduces to word-level bitwise XOR.
Union-Find decoder
Disjoint-set forest with union by rank and path compression. Clusters grow until each holds an even number of defects, then a spanning forest is peeled from the leaves inward to recover the correction. Near-linear: O(N α(N)).
Exact MWPM
Minimum-weight perfect matching over the defect graph, with pairwise distances from BFS shortest-path trees. It is exact at any number of defects, which took three attempts to make true. It first called the greedy decoder above sixteen defects and said nothing; then it ran a branch-and-bound search capped at twenty. Both caps bit hardest exactly where the problem is hard, so the decoder labelled exact was in practice the worst of the three at d = 9. It now runs Edmonds' blossom algorithm, which contracts the odd cycles that make general-graph matching hard, searches the contracted graph, and expands them again. Against Union-Find over 8,000 shots at each of eighteen code, noise and distance combinations, it is lighter in every one. Every array it uses is sized before the search starts and never grows, so a bug in the algorithm shows up as a wrong answer the tests catch rather than as a machine that runs out of memory.
Greedy decoder
Pairs each defect with its nearest unmatched partner or the closest boundary. Fastest and least accurate; useful mainly as a floor to measure the others against.
XZZX surface code
Every stabilizer measures alternating X and Z operators around its plaquette (X on one diagonal, Z on the other), which under biased noise decouples the decoding problem and lifts the threshold. Getting this to work took three fixes; the third is described under XZZX under a circuit, further down this list. The decoder derived its defects once and then matched them twice, on two graphs with different edge-to-qubit mappings, so every defect was explained once with X operators and once with Z and both corrections applied; a single X error returned the correct one-qubit X correction plus two invented Z ones, and their number grew with the lattice. It now builds one graph carrying both edge families with a per-edge type tag and matches once. Separately, the logical-operator check compared the residual against a hard-coded alternating string of Paulis that did not represent a logical operator of this lattice, so weight-one residuals, which cannot be logical errors in any code of distance 3 or more, were counted as failures at every distance. That is why the error rate came out flat instead of falling. The check now reduces the residual against a row-reduced basis of the stabilizer group and asks whether anything is left, which needs no convention at all. Brute-force search over the actual group confirms the construction was always sound: minimum logical weight 3 at d = 3 and above 4 at d = 5. After both fixes, no single X, Z or Y error causes a logical failure at any distance, the rate falls with distance at every bias, and XZZX beats the rotated code under bias as it should. That is a data-noise result, which is the regime the figure in section 8 measures.
Noise models
Pure data noise with perfect readout; phenomenological noise adding independent measurement errors per round over T = d rounds; and circuit-level noise that attributes faults to the individual gates of the extraction circuit. The final round is noiseless in every model: defects are time differences, so a lie in the last round has no following round to cancel against and leaves an unpaired defect the decoder must match somewhere. Including it made the code get worse with distance far below threshold, with logical error rising from 1.9% at d = 3 to 10.9% at d = 7 at p = 0.5%. Bias, located erasure, spatial bursts, and slow temporal drift layer on top.
Circuit-level noise
Faults are attributed to individual gates of the extraction circuit, which makes them qualitatively harder than the other two models: a fault on an ancilla partway through its four CNOTs propagates onto every data qubit it has yet to touch, so one fault becomes a correlated multi-qubit data error, known as a hook error. The phenomenological graph has no edge for that, so the matcher used to explain it with two unrelated edges and could walk the correction into a logical operator. About one single fault in thirty did exactly that, and the code got worse with distance rather than better. This is now decoded against a detector error model derived from the circuit instead: every elementary fault is propagated as a Pauli frame, and the detectors it fires together with the data error it leaves behind become one edge. Edges may correct several qubits at once, which is what the old graph could not express.
Building the model
Two things make it tractable. The circuit is defined once, as round_program, and both the stabilizer simulation and the fault propagation consume that same list. A model describing a subtly different circuit from the one being run is worse than no model, and two hand-written copies would not stay in step. And the decomposition that usually makes this hard is free here: a fault firing three or more detectors normally has to be split into graph-like pieces, but the decoder already matches twice, once per Pauli type, so a fault splits into its X part and its Z part and each is graph-like on its own. Measured over the whole enumeration, no fault fires more than two detectors in either graph. That second argument is a CSS one and does not carry to the XZZX code, which has a single stabilizer family and matches once; there the decomposition has to be made explicit.
Checking it
A distance-d code must survive any single fault, which makes for a test that is exhaustive and deterministic rather than statistical. Every fault the circuit admits is propagated, decoded against the model, corrected, and checked: 0 failures out of 600, 3,240 and 9,408 faults at d = 3, 5, 7, for Union-Find and exact MWPM alike. That test caught two further bugs. It showed the two CNOT schedules had to be transposes of each other the other way round from the pairing first tried, which passes at d = 5 and d = 7 and fails 44 times out of 600 at d = 3, so sampling would likely have missed it. And it caught state-preparation noise being applied before the baseline round, where an error sits in both readings a detector compares, never fires it, and lands in the residual uncorrectable at any distance. With both fixed the logical error rate at d = 3 scales as p² as theory demands, and a threshold appears where it should. The collapse fit of section 7, run four times independently, puts it at 0.41% ± 0.03. That is the least precise of the three models, for a good reason: circuit-level is much the slowest to simulate, so its sweep affords 1,200 shots a point where data noise gets 20,000, and the precision is limited by that rather than by the fit. Its exponent is not determined at that shot count, for the reason the aside in section 7 gives. The XZZX model is held to the same bar and passes it: 0 failures out of 672, 3,600 and 10,416 faults at d = 3, 5, 7, both decoders. There the test caught a third bug, settling a schedule that could not be reasoned out (see below).
XZZX under a circuit
The model above is built for a CSS code. A rotated plaquette measures either X or Z, so the two error families light disjoint detectors, the decoder matches twice (once per family), and that split is also what makes the decomposition free. An XZZX plaquette reads X Z Z X from a single ancilla: one syndrome bit, and both families firing the same detectors. No such split exists. This pairing, XZZX with circuit-level noise, was the last thing here that was simply wrong. It had received none of the XZZX fixes, so it still matched two graphs and scored against the hard-coded string, and its logical error rate rose with distance: 4.9% at d = 3 to 40.6% at d = 7, at p = 0.2%. It now builds one graph over a single node set, each edge naming the X part and the Z part of the correction it implies. Only X and Z faults are enumerated, never Y. Propagation is linear over the frame, so a Y fault is exactly the composition of the two, and letting the matcher pick both graphlike edges reconstructs it. That is the decomposition the CSS model got for free. The circuit holds every ancilla in |+> and reads an X leg with a CNOT, a Z leg with a CZ.

The two sublattices have to walk their neighbours in transposed orders. This is the same lesson the rotated code's N and Z schedules teach, and it was no more guessable the second time. With any single order shared by both, all 24 permutations leave 10 to 12 of 672 single faults uncorrectable at d = 3, while d = 5 stays clean, so only the exhaustive check at the smallest distance finds it. Searching the two sublattices independently over all 576 combinations, scored on commutation against the tableau and on the single-fault check, leaves exactly 6 that pass. All 6 are transposes.

Its threshold comes out 0.42% ± 0.04 against the rotated code's 0.41% ± 0.03, mean and spread over four independent sweeps each. On this evidence the two are not separated, and at 1,200 shots a point they will not be. The extra noise it carries shows up below threshold instead. Every XZZX ancilla needs an H where the rotated code rotates only its X-type ones (72 noise locations per round at d = 3 against 64), and at p = 0.26%, d = 7 that costs about 30% in logical error rate: 0.74% against 0.58% over 40,000 shots, which is roughly the size 12% more locations would predict.

The bias advantage does survive the circuit. At d = 7, p = 0.3%, going from η = 1 to η = 100 takes XZZX from 1.08% to 0.25% while the rotated code goes the other way, 1.07% to 2.37%. That is better than nine-fold apart, where at η = 1 the two were level. This entry previously reported the opposite, on the strength of a rotated simulator that counted only the logical error its preparation could see. Under strong Z-bias the failures it was missing are exactly the ones bias produces, so the rotated code appeared to improve. A measurement blind to half its outcomes does not just lose precision; it can invert the answer.
Located loss
An erasure is a qubit the hardware knows it lost. That knowledge is worth a great deal: the Pauli is uniform and unknown, but the location is not, so every decoding edge that location could have produced costs nothing and the matcher is free to route through it. Under circuit-level noise the location is a point in the circuit rather than a qubit, and an erasure on an ancilla partway through its CNOTs frees everything the loss goes on to touch, by the same propagation that makes a hook error. The model already knows which edges each circuit location produces, so it answers that directly. The effect is what the physics says it should be: at p = 0.8% and d = 5, logical error falls from 17.9% with no erasure to 5.6% when half the faults are located and to 0.02% when all of them are. Fully located noise has its own threshold near p = 2%, some four times the Pauli threshold. That it still has a threshold is the check that the information is being used rather than assumed.
Detection events
Decoders do not match raw syndrome readings; they match the places a check changed since the previous round, outcome ^ previous. Every figure on this page lights plaquettes on that quantity, which is what makes a lying readout legible as a pair in time. With a single round the previous outcome is zero by definition and the two coincide, so sections 3 to 5 are unaffected.
Logical channel
Under Pauli noise and a Pauli decoder the logical qubit sees a Pauli channel, which shrinks each Bloch axis independently, so the right characterisation is the diagonal of its transfer matrix: three factors in [−1, 1] that are all 1 when the channel does nothing. The previous version ran three identical simulations, differing in nothing but the variable each result was assigned to, and reported 1 − 2·failure_rate for each as though they were the components of a state. At zero noise that gave the "vector" (1, 1, 1), of length √3, which is outside the Bloch sphere. The simulators now return which logical Pauli class survived rather than a bare pass/fail, so the four channel probabilities can be counted and the shrink factors computed properly.

That fix was at first only half applied, which an audit of the numbers caught: four of the six code-and-noise pairings still returned a bare pass/fail, and the channel built from them came back with r_x pinned at exactly 1, meaning every failure was counted as the same kind. There were two causes. The XZZX simulators asked their stabilizer group only whether the residual was a logical operator, not which one; they now derive a pair of anticommuting logical representatives (the null space of the commutation map, reduced modulo the stabilizer group) and read the class off by commutation, so nothing is hard-coded. And the circuit-level simulator asked the tableau, which can only answer the question its basis poses: prepared in |0_L> it sees a logical X and is blind to a logical Z, an operator it commutes with. A Pauli frame now shadows the tableau through the same circuit and gives both at once. The derived representatives are checked against the rotated code's own independently known logicals (a column of X, a row of Z) over four thousand random residuals at d = 3 and d = 5.
Randomness
Shot seeds come from SplitMix64 over a counter, itself seeded from crypto.getRandomValues when the module is instantiated, so the figures re-sample on every reload rather than replaying one canned run. This was, until recently, the single largest defect in the engine: the WebAssembly build drew Xorshift::new(12345) inside each shot, making every shot of a batch bit-identical and collapsing every reported rate to a step function. Seeding one generator per shot from the previous shot's state is not enough either, because that hands each shot its predecessor's stream offset by a single draw, and batch variance comes out several times larger than binomial. Measured after the fix, over twelve repeats at d = 5, p = 5%, N = 4000: observed σ 0.00224 against a binomial 0.00222, a ratio of 1.01.
Threading
Two engine instances, seeded independently. The main thread keeps one for the interactive figures so clicks answer immediately; a worker holds a second for Monte Carlo, so a long sweep never blocks the page. All sampling happens inside Rust; the browser only says how many shots to run.
Statistics
Intervals are Wilson score intervals at 95%, which stay sensible when the observed rate is at or near zero. The collapse fit is a χ²-weighted quadratic least squares over the scaling variable, grid-searched over (p_th, ν).
Also available
The module exports three diagnostics alongside the simulation calls, which is how the claims on this page were checked rather than asserted: wasm_circuit_single_fault_test runs the exhaustive check and returns the faults tested and the number uncorrected; wasm_circuit_model_stats reports how many faults fire zero, one, two or more detectors, which is what says whether the graph can express them; and wasm_noise_slots gives the noise locations in one round, which is how the two codes' thresholds were compared on equal terms. Two more, wasm_match_cost_ptr and wasm_match_solve, hand the exact matcher to anything that wants it, which is how the independent toric-code cross-check in tools/ got a decoder good enough to compare against. The same crate also builds PyO3 bindings for offline benchmarking (see run_benchmarks.py), and the Rust test suite carries the schedule search and the frame-against-tableau comparison.
