Keeping a qubit alive

A qubit cannot be copied and cannot be looked at. Both of the things that make classical error correction easy are forbidden. Quantum error correction is the workaround — and everything below is a real stabilizer simulator running in your browser, not a recording of one.

01

The problem

Protecting classical information is easy because you are allowed to cheat. Store three copies of a bit, read all three, take the majority. Both moves — copying and reading — are free.

Neither is available for a qubit. The no-cloning theorem forbids duplicating an unknown quantum state, so there is nothing to take a majority over. And measuring a qubit collapses it: the superposition you were trying to protect is destroyed by the act of checking on it. A qubit in the state you care about is a qubit you are not allowed to inspect.

Worse, the errors are continuous. A classical bit fails one way — it flips. A qubit can rotate by any angle on the Bloch sphere, in any direction. There are infinitely many ways to be slightly wrong.

Quantum error correction gets around all three at once, and the trick is the same trick each time: never look at the data. Look only at relationships between qubits.

02

Parity, not state

Here is the move that makes everything else possible. Instead of measuring a qubit, measure whether a group of qubits agrees with itself — their parity. The answer is a single bit: even or odd. It says nothing whatsoever about what any individual qubit holds, so it collapses nothing you cared about.

Four qubits, one measurement. Click them.

Figure 1 · one stabilizer Z ⊗ Z ⊗ Z ⊗ Z

That is the bargain. The measurement is safe precisely because it is blind — and being blind, it leaves you with an ambiguity you will have to resolve some other way. Hold onto that. It comes back in section 05 as the central difficulty of the whole field.

On continuous errors This also quietly solves the third problem. Measuring a stabilizer projects a continuously rotated qubit onto either "no error" or "a discrete Pauli error". The infinity of possible wrongness collapses into a handful of cases the moment you check. Correcting X, Y, and Z is enough to correct everything.
03

Anatomy of a patch

One parity check protects nothing on its own. Tile the plane with them and something useful appears. The rotated surface code puts d² data qubits on a grid and draws a checkerboard of parity checks across it: Z-plaquettes that catch bit flips, X-plaquettes that catch phase flips.

There are d² − 1 checks constraining d² qubits. The single degree of freedom left over is the logical qubit — spread across the whole patch, stored in no particular place, and therefore not destroyable by damaging any particular place.

Hover anything below to see what watches what.

Figure 2 · the lattice read-only

The distance d is the length of the shortest path across the patch, and it sets how many individual failures it takes to destroy the logical qubit: you need at least d of them, arranged in a line. That is the entire value proposition. Everything after this is about making that line unlikely.

04

Errors and syndromes

Now break it. Click a data qubit to inject an error and watch which checks fire. The pattern of fired checks is called the syndrome, and it is the only information you ever get — the errors themselves are, by construction, invisible.

Then press Grow a chain and watch what happens. A run of adjacent errors lights up only its two endpoints. Every check in the middle sees two errors, reads even parity, and stays quiet.

Inject
An erasure is a qubit known to have been lost. It fires no check on its own — what it hands the decoder is the location.

This is the fact the rest of the page is built on. The syndrome sees the boundary of an error chain, never the chain itself. Two defects tell you that something connects them. They do not tell you what.

05

Decoding is a guess

A decoder takes the syndrome and proposes a correction: some chain of errors with the same endpoints. Any such chain will silence every check. The decoder has no way to know whether it picked the one that actually happened, so it picks the most probable — usually, the shortest.

Remarkably, it does not need to be right. Apply the correction on top of the true error and look at what is left over, the residual. If the residual closes into loops, it acts trivially on the logical qubit and you have won despite guessing wrong. Only if the residual runs all the way across the patch have you lost — and then every check reads quiet while the answer is silently wrong.

Press Long chain a few times at d = 3. Once the chain is longer than half the patch, the shortest way to close it is the wrong way round, the decoder takes it, and the residual becomes a logical operator. This is what a logical error is: not a failure to find a consistent correction, but a confident and consistent wrong answer.

The three decoders trade accuracy against speed. Union-Find grows clusters until each contains an even number of defects and peels them, in near-linear time. Exact minimum-weight perfect matching finds the genuinely optimal pairing and costs a great deal more. Greedy just pairs nearest neighbours and is the fastest and worst.

06

When the readouts lie

Everything so far assumed the syndrome was trustworthy. It is not. The parity checks are themselves small quantum circuits on real hardware, and they misreport. A single lying readout is indistinguishable from a real error — and "correcting" an error that never happened introduces one.

The fix is to measure repeatedly and decode the whole history at once. What the decoder actually matches is not the raw readings but the places they change between one round and the next — the detection events. Under that lens the two failure modes separate. A data error flips a check once and leaves it flipped, giving two events side by side in one layer. A lying readout disagrees with itself for a single round, giving two events on the same check in consecutive layers: a pair separated in time rather than in space.

The plaquettes below light on detection events, so this is the decoder's own view. With a single round the two notions coincide, which is why the earlier figures did not have to draw the distinction.

This costs you: d rounds of measurement per logical operation, and a decoding problem that grows in three dimensions instead of two. It is also non-negotiable. Every figure before this one was describing an idealisation.

07

The threshold

Everything above is machinery. This is the question it exists to answer: if I add more physical qubits, does the logical qubit get better?

The answer is not always yes, and that is the entire subtlety. A bigger patch corrects longer error chains — but it also has more qubits to go wrong, and more checks to misreport. Below some critical physical error rate the first effect wins and logical error falls off exponentially in d. Above it, the second wins and adding qubits makes things actively worse. That crossing point is the threshold.

Notice that the two noise models cross over in different places. Perfect measurements buy you a much higher threshold than faulty ones — which is why the honest number to quote for hardware is always the phenomenological or circuit-level one.

To locate the crossing precisely, sweep p across all three distances and fit. Near threshold the curves for every distance collapse onto a single universal curve when plotted against (p − p_th) · d1/ν; the pair of values that makes the collapse tightest is the threshold and its critical exponent.

Reading the fit The reduced χ² tells you whether the collapse is believable. Near 1 means the scaling ansatz describes the data within its error bars. Much larger means the fit is being forced — usually because the sweep reaches too far from threshold, where the universal form stops applying.
08

Biased noise

Real devices do not produce X, Y, and Z errors in equal measure. In most superconducting and spin qubits, dephasing dominates — Z errors can be one or two orders of magnitude more common than bit flips. A code designed for symmetric noise is leaving that structure on the table.

The XZZX variant alternates X and Z operators around every plaquette instead of using separate X-type and Z-type checks. The published result is that under strong bias its decoding problem decouples into near-independent one-dimensional chains, lifting the threshold substantially.

It took three fixes to get this simulator to show it. Its XZZX decoder used to match one defect set twice, on two graphs with different edge mappings, so a pure X error came back with invented Z corrections attached — more of them the larger the patch. And the logical-operator check used a hard-coded string of Paulis that was simply wrong, so it reported failures for residuals that were not logical operators at all, at every distance. Both are fixed: the two error families are matched together in one pass, and whether a residual is a logical error is now decided by asking the stabilizer group directly.

The result is the one the literature describes. Run the comparison: at strong bias XZZX pulls clearly ahead of the rotated code, and both now improve as the patch grows.

The logical channel

Under Pauli noise and a Pauli decoder the logical qubit sees a Pauli channel, which shrinks each axis of the Bloch sphere independently. The three factors below are those shrink amounts — the diagonal of the channel's transfer matrix. A noiseless channel leaves all three at 1 because it shrinks nothing; noise the decoder cannot undo squashes the sphere toward a point.

On circuit-level noise Circuit-level is the only mode here that is not yet a fair test of the code. Two real bugs in it are fixed — every shot used to replay identical measurement outcomes, and the X and Z ancillas walked their plaquettes in the same order, so the two extraction circuits did not commute and a noiseless circuit reported 100% logical error at d = 5. Both are gone: a noiseless circuit now never fails, at any distance, and the curve rises with p as it should. But the decoder still matches on the phenomenological spacetime graph, which has no edges for the correlated two-qubit errors a failing CNOT produces mid-extraction. Without those, larger patches are penalised rather than helped, and no threshold appears. Section 10 has the detail.
10

Under the hood

Nothing on this page is precomputed. Every number was produced by a Rust stabilizer simulator compiled to WebAssembly and executed on your machine while you read.

Stabilizer engine
A packed binary symplectic tableau. By the Gottesman–Knill theorem, Clifford circuits on n qubits are classically simulable in polynomial time; propagating Paulis through the tableau costs O(n²) and reduces to word-level bitwise XOR.
Union-Find decoder
Disjoint-set forest with union by rank and path compression. Clusters grow until each holds an even number of defects, then a spanning forest is peeled from the leaves inward to recover the correction. Near-linear: O(N α(N)).
Exact MWPM
Optimal minimum-weight perfect matching over the defect graph, with pairwise distances from BFS shortest-path trees. Capped at 16 defects — beyond that the exact search is too slow to run interactively.
Greedy decoder
Pairs each defect with its nearest unmatched partner or the closest boundary. Fastest and least accurate; useful mainly as a floor to measure the others against.
XZZX surface code
Every stabilizer measures alternating X and Z operators around its plaquette — X on one diagonal, Z on the other — which under biased noise decouples the decoding problem and lifts the threshold. Getting this to work took two fixes. The decoder derived its defects once and then matched them twice, on two graphs with different edge-to-qubit mappings, so every defect was explained once with X operators and once with Z and both corrections applied; a single X error returned the correct one-qubit X correction plus two invented Z ones, and their number grew with the lattice. It now builds one graph carrying both edge families with a per-edge type tag and matches once. Separately, the logical-operator check compared the residual against a hard-coded alternating string of Paulis that did not represent a logical operator of this lattice, so weight-one residuals — impossible logical errors in any code of distance 3 or more — were counted as failures at every distance, which is why the error rate came out flat instead of falling. That check now reduces the residual against a row-reduced basis of the stabilizer group and asks whether anything is left, which needs no convention at all. Brute-force search over the actual group confirms the construction was always sound: minimum logical weight 3 at d = 3 and above 4 at d = 5. After both fixes, no single X, Z or Y error causes a logical failure at any distance, the rate falls with distance at every bias, and XZZX beats the rotated code under bias as it should.
Noise models
Pure data noise with perfect readout; phenomenological noise adding independent measurement errors per round over T = d rounds; and circuit-level noise that attributes faults to the individual gates of the extraction circuit. The final round is noiseless in every model: defects are time differences, so a lie in the last round has no following round to cancel against and leaves an unpaired defect the decoder must match somewhere. Including it made the code get worse with distance far below threshold — logical error rising from 1.9% at d = 3 to 10.9% at d = 7 at p = 0.5%. Bias, located erasure, spatial bursts, and slow temporal drift layer on top.
Circuit-level noise
Two bugs fixed, one gap left. Every circuit-level shot used to replay the same measurement outcomes, because the stabilizer tableau seeded its generator from a literal in its constructor — so the entire run was deterministic and a noiseless circuit reported either 0% or 100% logical error depending only on the distance. Separately, X and Z ancillas walked their plaquettes in the same order, and boundary plaquettes compressed their two gates into the first two time slots; the two extraction circuits therefore did not commute where plaquettes overlap, and each measurement disturbed the other. Ancillas now interleave in opposite orders — the classic N and Z schedules — and are scheduled by direction rather than by index into a variable-length list. A noiseless circuit now fails never, at d = 3, 5, 7, and the curve rises monotonically with p. What is still missing: the decoder matches on the phenomenological spacetime graph, which carries no edges for the correlated two-qubit errors a CNOT failing mid-extraction produces. Without those hook-error edges the decoder mis-corrects in a way that grows with the patch, so distance still costs more than it buys and no threshold appears — measured down to p = 2×10⁻⁵.
Detection events
Decoders do not match raw syndrome readings; they match the places a check changed since the previous round, outcome ^ previous. Every figure on this page lights plaquettes on that quantity, which is what makes a lying readout legible as a pair in time. With a single round the previous outcome is zero by definition and the two coincide, so sections 3 to 5 are unaffected.
Logical channel
Under Pauli noise and a Pauli decoder the logical qubit sees a Pauli channel, which shrinks each Bloch axis independently, so the honest characterisation is the diagonal of its transfer matrix — three factors in [−1, 1] that are all 1 when the channel does nothing. The previous version ran three identical simulations, differing in nothing but the variable each result was assigned to, and reported 1 − 2·failure_rate for each as though they were the components of a state. At zero noise that gave the "vector" (1, 1, 1), of length √3 — outside the Bloch sphere. The simulators now return which logical Pauli class survived rather than a bare pass/fail, so the four channel probabilities can be counted and the shrink factors computed properly.
Randomness
Shot seeds come from SplitMix64 over a counter, itself seeded from crypto.getRandomValues when the module is instantiated — so the figures re-sample on every reload rather than replaying one canned run. This was, until recently, the single largest defect in the engine: the WebAssembly build drew Xorshift::new(12345) inside each shot, making every shot of a batch bit-identical and collapsing every reported rate to a step function. Seeding one generator per shot from the previous shot's state is not enough either — that hands each shot its predecessor's stream offset by a single draw, and batch variance comes out several times larger than binomial. Measured after the fix, over twelve repeats at d = 5, p = 5%, N = 4000: observed σ 0.00224 against a binomial 0.00222, a ratio of 1.01.
Threading
Two engine instances, seeded independently. The main thread keeps one for the interactive figures so clicks answer immediately; a worker holds a second for Monte Carlo, so a long sweep never blocks the page. All sampling happens inside Rust — the browser only says how many shots to run.
Statistics
Intervals are Wilson score intervals at 95%, which stay sensible when the observed rate is at or near zero. The collapse fit is a χ²-weighted quadratic least squares over the scaling variable, grid-searched over (p_th, ν).
